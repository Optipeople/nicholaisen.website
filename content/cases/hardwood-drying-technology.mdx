## The operation nobody looks at

In most solid wood operations, drying is the cycle nobody looks at carefully. The kilns run, the wood comes out, and the downstream line either copes or does not. When it does not, the assumption is usually that the wood was the problem, not the schedule.

In this case, the schedule was the problem. The original schedule had been written for a different mix of species and a different downstream tolerance, and had been updated piecemeal over a decade without a structured rebuild. The kiln operators knew it was not optimal. They did not have the data to prove it.

<Callout title="Why the data was the unlock" tone="info">
The operations director's conservatism about kiln changes was rational. A bad drying decision can ruin a batch worth tens of thousands of euros. The instrumentation gave him a reason to authorize a controlled trial, because for the first time the proposed change was defensible against a baseline.
</Callout>

## What we measured

We installed continuous moisture probes at three depths in two kilns, plus surface temperature and air velocity logging. On the planer-moulder line we instrumented for the first time, classifying every rework event by suspected cause: dimensional variation, surface defect, internal stress release, and a small number of other categories. After six weeks we had enough data to correlate the late-cycle ramp segment of the original schedule with a specific dimensional rework pattern downstream. The mechanism turned out to be a too-aggressive late-cycle moisture differential between core and surface.

## What we changed

The revised schedule extended the late-cycle equalization phase by approximately fourteen hours and reduced the peak surface temperature by three degrees. The trade-off was a slightly longer total cycle, partially offset by a small reduction in the conditioning phase. Net cycle time grew by under four percent. The yield and rework gains, measured downstream, more than absorbed the small cycle penalty.

<PullQuote attribution="Operations director">
I had refused this change for years because nobody could show me what would actually happen inside the kiln. Once we could measure it, the answer became obvious in three weeks.
</PullQuote>

## A note on transferability

We are careful not to claim that this revised schedule will work for every hardwood producer. The right schedule depends on species mix, kiln type, downstream tolerance and finishing process. What is transferable is the method: instrument first, correlate to downstream, then change with evidence.
