## The benchmark trap

The most-cited OEE benchmarks in our industry are essentially useless. The number 85 percent gets quoted as world-class, 60 percent as average, and anything below 50 percent as a problem to fix. We have read those numbers in three different consultancy reports this year, all citing each other.

The numbers are not wrong, exactly. They are just not measuring the same thing across the plants being compared. One plant counts planned changeovers as downtime; another counts them as planned and excludes them from the denominator. One plant runs three eight-hour shifts; another runs two ten-hour shifts plus weekend overtime treated as bonus. One plant calculates quality on first-pass yield; another on net-shipped yield after rework. The headline numbers look comparable. They are not.

## What credible OEE measurement looks like

A credible OEE program in a wood or panel plant has six characteristics. We look for all of them when we audit.

<Callout title="The six characteristics" tone="accent">
First, downtime is classified at the source by the operator, not reconstructed the next morning. Second, the category list is short enough that the operator can pick correctly in under twenty seconds. Third, planned downtime is explicit and visible, not buried. Fourth, performance is measured against a defended ideal cycle time, not a vendor brochure number. Fifth, quality is first-pass, not net-shipped. Sixth, the same definition is used every week, every month, every quarter. No definition drift.
</Callout>

If any of those six are absent, the OEE number on the screen is decorative. It is not a management tool. It might be a fine decoration; we are not against decoration. Just do not act on it.

## The number is not the goal

The goal of an OEE program is not to make the number go up. It is to give the team a credible signal so the right improvements get prioritized. If the signal is honest and the improvement cadence is disciplined, the number tends to go up by itself, by roughly eight to eighteen points over twelve months on most lines we work with.

If the goal becomes the number, the number gets gamed. We have seen the planned-downtime category quietly expand. We have seen the ideal cycle time quietly degraded so performance looks better. We have seen quality metrics calculated only on the products that were running well that week. The result is a plant where the OEE chart goes up and the actual throughput stays flat. That is the worst kind of measurement program: one that consumes attention without producing improvement.

## What good plants do differently

The plants we work with that have OEE programs that actually work share three habits. They review the OEE definition annually and stand by it for the rest of the year. They run a tight twenty-minute weekly meeting where the previous week's OEE drives a small set of named actions. And they are willing to publish the ugly weeks. A plant that hides its bad weeks is not running a credible program; it is running a PR exercise.

<PullQuote attribution="A plant manager whose OEE went from 51 to 69 over fourteen months">
The first six weeks were embarrassing. The number was honest for the first time in years and it was lower than anything we had ever reported. We had to choose: hide the number or fix the line. We fixed the line.
</PullQuote>

## Practical advice

If you are starting an OEE program, do not benchmark your starting number against the consultancy reports. Benchmark it against your own honest baseline at week one and look for ten points of improvement over six months. That is a real number. The 85-percent world-class figure is not.

If you have an existing OEE program, audit it against the six characteristics above. If it fails on three or more, the program is not measuring what it claims to measure. You are better off taking it down and starting over than continuing to make decisions on a misleading signal.
