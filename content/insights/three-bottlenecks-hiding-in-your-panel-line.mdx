## The constraint is rarely where you think

When a plant manager tells us their line is bottlenecked at the nesting cell, we believe them less than half the time. Not because the plant manager is wrong about what they see. They are right about what they see. The constraint is real. But it is usually a symptom, and the cause is somewhere upstream or somewhere quieter.

Forty production audits in panel and furniture facilities across Northern Europe have given us a short list of the places we look first. None of them is glamorous.

## Bottleneck one: the edgebanding changeover

Edgebanding is the most common silent bottleneck on a panel line. The machine itself is rarely the problem. The changeover is.

A typical edgebander changeover should run in seven to ten minutes if the machine is well set up and the operator follows a structured procedure. We routinely walk into plants where changeovers run twenty to thirty minutes and have done so for years. Nobody has measured it because changeovers are not classified as downtime in the dashboard. They are classified as setup, which is treated as planned, which is treated as free.

<Callout title="A test you can run tomorrow" tone="info">
Stand at the edgebander on a day with at least four product changeovers. Time each one with a stopwatch. Total the changeover time as a percentage of the planned shift hours. If the answer is over ten percent, you have a bottleneck the dashboard is not telling you about.
</Callout>

## Bottleneck two: the case-clamp handover

The handover between the upstream machining cell and the case-clamping or assembly cell is the second-most-common silent bottleneck. The symptom is a buffer of WIP that grew without anyone deciding it should. The cause is usually a mismatch between the upstream cycle and the downstream takt, plus a manual transfer that absorbs the difference invisibly.

Three things follow from this. First, the upstream cell starves the downstream more than the report suggests, because the buffer hides it. Second, the WIP itself ties up cash and floor space. Third, when something does go wrong upstream, the buffer delays the signal to the downstream team by hours.

The fix is rarely a new machine. It is usually a smaller buffer, a clearer pull signal, and a layout adjustment that makes the handover a flow instead of an interruption.

## Bottleneck three: nesting yield

This one is not a throughput bottleneck in the strict sense. It is a margin bottleneck that masquerades as a cost line.

<MetricRow metrics={[{"label": "Typical yield", "value": "84-88%"}, {"label": "Achievable yield", "value": "90-93%"}, {"label": "Margin recovery", "value": "1-3 pts"}, {"label": "Capex", "value": "Often zero"}]} />

Most panel producers we have measured run nesting yield in the 84 to 88 percent range and treat that as the natural ceiling. With careful work on part rotation, remnant management, and the small CAM-side decisions about cut sequencing, we have repeatedly moved that ceiling to 90 to 93 percent. The capex required is often zero. The discipline required is non-trivial.

## How to find your version

The pattern across these three is the same. The bottleneck is not in the machine that runs loudest. It is in the operation that has been treated as fine for years because nobody measured it sharply. The way to find your version is to instrument honestly for four weeks, classify downtime at the source, and walk the floor with a stopwatch for at least one full shift.

<PullQuote attribution="A production manager, after his first honest audit">
We have been buying capacity at the wrong end of the line for five years. I want to undo most of the last three capex decisions.
</PullQuote>

You do not have to undo the last three capex decisions. But you might want to look hard at the next one before you sign it.
